**The problem it solves**

**The Problem: The Flaw of Reactive Governance**

Road and transport safety is one of the most critical civic challenges in India, claiming over 1.5 lakh lives every year. Traditional municipal traffic management is fundamentally crippled by a reactive loop. By the time the issue is resolved, the damage is already done, lives are lost, and hours of citizen productivity are wasted. Traffic lights operate on dumb, static countdown timers oblivious to real vehicle queues, and police deployment happens after a hotspot has already turned fatal.


**The Solution: Answering the Proactive Question**

RoadSense AI flips this paradigm entirely. Instead of waiting for disaster to strike, our system acts on the proactive side. Based on live vehicle kinematics, temporal patterns, and environmental factors, it determines and predicts where the next critical accident hotspot or bottleneck will emerge, and how do we dynamically intervene right now to prevent it from ever happening?

**RoadSense AI answers this through an end-to-end intelligence ecosystem:**

**Real-Time Vision & Kinematics**: Using YOLOv11 detection and ByteTrack multi-object tracking on live CCTV streams, the system monitors  traffic flow, calculating true ground-plane speeds and spatial density via calibrated 4-point perspective homography.

**Dynamic Adaptive Signal Control (DTSC)**: Rather than relying on rigid fixed-timer green lights, our dynamic algorithm calculates real-time green lights to track and monitor traffic violations, serving as a data parameter.

**Longitudinal ML Risk Forecasting**: A leakage-free Supervised ML Risk Forecaster (Random Forest with strict chronological temporal validation) analyzes 50 municipal zones over 52 weeks to forecast future accident probabilities, alerting authorities to deploy preventative patrols and speed-calming measures days before accidents occur.

**Autonomous AI Safety Briefings**: An integrated LLM briefing engine (Llama-3.3-70B / Gemini-2.5-Flash) translates complex kinematic matrices and predictive probabilities into plain-English action-ready guidelines and briefings.

**Flexible Architecture (The highlight of our project)**: 
To bridge the gap between municipal authorities and everyday commuters, RoadSense AI features a flexible public API promoting code visibility. We empower external developers, urban researchers, and civic hackers to build commuter alert apps, navigation aids, and third-party civic tools without reinventing the underlying vision or predictive infrastructure. To show an example we built a civilian frontend as well. 

**Local Admin Command Center (Streamlit)**: An analytical powerhouse built for traffic controllers and municipal planners. It delivers deep telemetry, live CCTV calibration controls, DTSC signal overrides, machine learning diagnostics, and predictive zone ranking leaderboards.

**Civilian Transparency Portal (Web Frontend)**: A lightweight, fast, accessible interface built for the general public. It provides citizens with transparent, real-time visibility into crossroad telemetry, current signal phases, queue states, and road safety indices without overwhelming administrative complexity.

**Challenges we ran into**

**Scarcity of Public Indian Road Datasets**: Unlike Western cities with granular open-data portals, structured zone-by-zone Indian traffic accident and speed telemetry is virtually non-existent in the public domain. We had to invest immense effort researching municipal patterns to establish baseline benchmarks.

**Simulating Databases for Chaotic Indian Road Realities**: Indian roads cannot be modeled using generic simulators designed for lane-disciplined Western traffic. We had to build a custom simulation pipeline (sim.py) that models high-density two-wheeler swarms, auto-rickshaws, erratic lane adherence, festival surges, and monsoon weather severity across 50 municipal sectors.

**Calibrating Predictive Factor Weightage**: Balancing feature weights was a major machine learning challenge. Determining how much influence variables like vehicle density, average speed delta, heavy-vehicle ratio, road surface condition, and weather severity should hold required extensive correlation analyses, SHAP evaluations, and hyperparameter tuning to avoid bias.

**Perspective Homography Calibration**: Converting 2D CCTV pixel coordinates into accurate real-world metric speeds required mapping arbitrary camera angles to a bird's-eye ground plane via 4-point quad homography transforms across varying roadway geometries.

**Eliminating Temporal Data Leakage**: Standard random cross-validation catastrophically leaks future trends into past training on time-series panel data. We engineered a strict chronological split along with rolling and lagged temporal feature extractors to ensure the risk forecaster remains strictly causal and predictive.

# Technical Challenges

Building Pounce was far more than creating another e-commerce website. It required solving engineering challenges across **real-time graphics, networking, AI, machine learning, and distributed systems**. Every feature from a living 3D shopping world to AI employees and secure background automation had to work together seamlessly while remaining responsive and scalable for thousands of concurrent users.

- **Building a Living 3D World**  
  Creating a realistic shopping experience using **Three.js** was one of the biggest challenges. We had to build an optimized 3D environment with interactive stores, realistic lighting, animated products, and NPCs that continuously walk around, browse items, and perform natural actions so the world feels alive instead of static.

- **Character Animation & Movement**  
  Designing player avatars with smooth walking, running, idle, and interaction animations required careful animation blending and synchronization. Every movement had to feel natural while remaining responsive across different devices.

- **Real-Time Multiplayer & Voice Communication**  
  Synchronizing multiple players inside the same virtual world with minimal latency was a major challenge. Player positions, animations, interactions, and **real-time speech-to-speech communication** all needed to stay perfectly synchronized to create a natural collaborative shopping experience.

- **Multi-Agent AI System**  
  Instead of relying on a single chatbot, we built a **multi-agent architecture** where each AI employee specializes in a specific tasks - greeter, sales associate, stockroom assistant, and cashier. Managing agent coordination, conversation context, and smooth handoffs between them required careful orchestration.

- **Real-Time AR Try-On**  
  Unlike traditional virtual try-on systems that rely on uploading an image, our goal was a **live AR experience**. This required machine learning models capable of real-time body and foot tracking, accurate product alignment, and low-latency rendering while users move naturally.

- **Scalable Background Workers**  
  Features like **Pounce Watcher** continuously monitor product availability and pricing. Designing a background worker architecture capable of processing thousands of monitoring jobs simultaneously without impacting user-facing services was a significant engineering challenge.

- **Redis, Queues & Distributed Processing**  
  Managing large-scale asynchronous workloads required careful use of **Redis**, distributed queues, caching, job retries, duplicate prevention, worker recovery, and queue prioritization to keep the system reliable under heavy traffic.

- **Performance & Scalability**  
  Bringing together real-time 3D rendering, multiplayer networking, AI agents, AR processing, voice communication, and background automation while maintaining a smooth experience for thousands of concurrent users required extensive optimization across the entire stack.

# The Problem

Online shopping today is a **solitary experience**. You scroll through endless product images with no shopkeeper to consult, no friend beside you, and little confidence that a product will meet your expectations. ## What is Pounce?

**Pounce** transforms online shopping into a **shared 3D retail experience**. Enter a virtual store with friends, interact with AI-powered staff, explore products from live merchant catalogs, try products in AR, and pay securely through **Prava**, ensuring every purchase stays within the limits you approve.

- **Secure Payments with Prava** — Approve purchases with a passkey while your card details remain hidden. Prava issues a **single-use credential** limited to one merchant and one approved amount, preventing unauthorized spending.

**Best Visa Intelligent Commerce Implementation**

Pounce's checkout is powered by **Visa Intelligent Commerce through Prava**, enabling AI agents to complete purchases without ever exposing a user's payment credentials. At checkout, users approve the purchase with a **passkey**, after which Prava issues a **single-use Visa payment credential** that is strictly **scoped to one merchant and one approved amount**. The AI agent completes the transaction using this credential, while the user's actual card details never reach either Pounce or the merchant.

The same security model extends to **autonomous purchasing** through **Pounce Watcher**. During setup, users authorize a **Visa-backed Prava mandate** with a one-time passkey, defining the maximum amount and the specific merchant the agent is allowed to purchase from. When the user's condition such as a price drop or product restock is met, the watcher securely executes the purchase within those predefined limits. Prava again generates a **single-use Visa credential**, allowing the AI agent to complete the checkout automatically without requiring another approval.

## Why It Matters

Traditional AI shopping agents require users to trust the model with their payment information. Pounce removes that trust assumption by building on **Prava's implementation of Visa Intelligent Commerce**, where security is enforced by the payment infrastructure itself rather than the AI agent.

Through **Prava**, users benefit from:

- **Card details are never shared** with either Pounce or the merchant.
- **Single-use Visa payment credentials** that expire immediately after a successful transaction.
- **Merchant- and amount-scoped credentials**, ensuring payments can only be used for the approved merchant and spending limit.
- **Visa-backed Prava mandates** for autonomous purchases, allowing payments only within the limits the user has pre-approved.
- **Passkey-based authorization**, ensuring users remain in control while AI agents securely complete the shopping workflow.

By combining Pounce's AI-powered shopping experience with **Prava's secure payment infrastructure built on Visa Intelligent Commerce**, agentic commerce becomes both autonomous and trustworthy.

**The problem it solves**

Crosslink solves the problem of delayed and disconnected missing-person searches. Today, information about a missing person may stay with one police station, so a sighting in another city or district may not be matched quickly. Crosslink connects these reports and sightings in one system and uses AI to identify possible matches in real time.

**Challenges we ran into**

Building CrossLink required solving several critical issues across vector mathematics, computer vision limitations, and frontend data handling.

Vector Metric Discrepancy: We initially configured pgvector and face-api.js to use Cosine similarity. We discovered that this metric ignores vector magnitude, crushing real human faces into a dangerously narrow band and causing massive false positives (22 out of 24 incorrect matches during testing). We had to drop the database index and migrate the entire matching architecture to Euclidean (L2) distance to achieve accurate cluster gaps.

Low-Resolution False Confidence: We found that small input faces lacked enough visual feature density, causing the recognition model to mathematically "guess" missing details and confidently match completely different strangers. We solved this by engineering a strict 110px dimensional guardrail that blocks low-quality crops before they hit the database.

Metadata Destruction: We originally planned to extract EXIF GPS data server-side, but realized our canvas re-encoding step (captureFrame()) permanently destroyed the tags. We had to shift EXIF extraction entirely to the client-side browser before processing. Furthermore, social platforms like WhatsApp strip metadata entirely, requiring us to build graceful fallback UI badges (no-gps, no-exif) instead of relying on guaranteed coordinates.

Atomic Deployment Synchronization: Overhauling the database structure—such as changing date inputs to timestamptz and rebuilding the HNSW vector index—required strict deployment sequencing. We had to carefully choreograph Supabase SQL migrations immediately before Vercel GitHub pushes to prevent the production app from crashing due to mismatched data types during live updates.

Edge Security Integration: Securing the admin dashboard and API routes without exposing credentials or leaving backdoors required running authentication at the network edge. We implemented Vercel Edge middleware utilizing HTTP-only cookies and HMAC-SHA256 signing to lock down the routes seamlessly.

**Challenges we ran into**

### 1. The VRAM Bottleneck (Single GPU Constraints)
**The Hurdle:** Our biggest challenge was running multiple heavy neural networks—like ConvNeXt V2 for spatial video analysis and Wav2Vec2 for acoustic dissection—on a single laptop GPU (an RTX 4050) without encountering CUDA Out-Of-Memory (OOM) fatal crashes. 
**How we got over it:** We designed a **"VRAM Hot-Swap Sequence."** Instead of trying to keep models loaded simultaneously, we built an asynchronous state machine. Once the video frame analysis finishes, the backend explicitly deletes the model, runs Python garbage collection (`gc.collect()`), and executes `torch.cuda.empty_cache()` to completely purge the GPU memory before rapidly loading the next model for the audio track. We used a localized SQLite DB to preserve the tracking coordinates during this handoff.

### 2. Database Race Conditions and Write Locks
**The Hurdle:** Because PATRONUS runs highly concurrent, multi-modal asynchronous tracks (analyzing video frames, audio frequencies, and API responses at the same time), using a single monolithic database resulted in massive write-locks, race conditions, and corrupted forensic logs.
**How we got over it:** We completely decentralized our data storage. We implemented an isolated micro-database architecture. Each forensics module writes strictly to its own dedicated SQLite instance (vid.db, img_forensics.db, text_forensics.db, etc.). This guaranteed parallel, zero-lock transactions and allowed all asynchronous tracks to save data safely.

### 3. High Latency During Live Inference
**The Hurdle:** Deepfake detection is computationally expensive. Running OCR, spatial bounding, and frequency analysis sequentially was taking too long, which would ruin the pacing of a live "Hacker Theatre" presentation. We needed execution times under 2 seconds.
**How we got over it:** We implemented a **Hybrid Dual-Track Framework**. We decoupled the workload: pure mathematical matrix transformations (like 2D-Discrete Cosine Transforms and STFT audio mapping) run locally on the RTX 4050, while simultaneously, the semantic reasoning and logic auditing (like analyzing document structures or phishing intent) are dispatched to the cloud via the **Gemini 2.5 Flash API**. Fusing local hardware with cloud cognitive reasoning dropped our execution latency to ~1.2 - 1.8 seconds per module.

### 4. Visualizing Complex Data (The "Hacker Theatre" UI)
**The Hurdle:** Returning raw JSON coordinates, NLP Perplexity scores, and Grad-CAM matrices to the user is visually boring and hard to understand for a non-technical audience.
**How we got over it:** We built a highly dynamic React frontend that translates math into intuition. ### LOOPZ
[![Devfolio](https://img.shields.io/badge/Devfolio-View%20Project-4B32C3?style=flat-square&logo=devfolio&logoColor=white)](https://devfolio.co/projects/loopz-c1eb) [![GitHub](https://img.shields.io/badge/GitHub-Source%20Code-181717?style=flat-square&logo=github&logoColor=white)](https://github.com/Shiv0087/loopz) [![Demo](https://img.shields.io/badge/Demo-Live-00C853?style=flat-square&logo=googlechrome&logoColor=white)](https://pypi.org/project/loopz/) [![Built at](https://img.shields.io/badge/Built%20at-Devlynix%20Buildathon%201.0-0052CC?style=flat-square)](https://devlynix-buildathon.devfolio.co) ![Likes](https://img.shields.io/badge/Likes-2-FF6B6B?style=flat-square)

> Never lose loop progress again.

![PyTorch](https://img.shields.io/badge/PyTorch-333333?style=flat-square) ![scikit-learn](https://img.shields.io/badge/scikit--learn-333333?style=flat-square) ![NumPy](https://img.shields.io/badge/NumPy-333333?style=flat-square) ![Python](https://img.shields.io/badge/Python-333333?style=flat-square) ![PyPi](https://img.shields.io/badge/PyPi-333333?style=flat-square)

**The problem it solves**

Every ML engineer, data scientist, and CS student has felt this exact moment. You are 6 hours into a training run. Loss is dropping. Accuracy is climbing. Then your Colab session disconnects, your laptop dies, or your server restarts — and you are back at epoch 1. Again.
This is not a rare edge case. This is the daily reality of anyone training models on free-tier cloud GPUs, shared university servers, or underpowered personal machines. Millions of GPU-hours are wasted every year re-doing work that was already done — not because the model was wrong, but because the infrastructure failed and there was no safety net.
loopz is that safety net.
It is a Python library that adds automatic crash recovery to any loop with a single decorator. You do not rewrite your training logic. You do not add boilerplate. You add @loopz.track(...) above your function and from that point every checkpoint is saved atomically. Crash at step 60,000 out of 100,000 — run the same script again and it resumes from exactly step 60,000.
For ML training, loopz saves and restores the complete training state: model weights, optimizer, learning rate scheduler, random seeds for deterministic resume, and any custom loop variables like running loss or best accuracy. The crash never happened as far as your training loop is concerned.
This project has no flashy frontend — and that is intentional. The interface for a developer tool is the terminal, the decorator, and the status output. The real deliverable is a working, published library that solves a problem every developer in this community has personally felt. It is live on PyPI right now — pip install loopz — built by a solo developer from India who lost one too many hours of Colab training.

**Who it helps:**
- ML students running experiments on Colab that disconnects
- Data engineers processing large datasets that can crash midway
- Researchers training models overnight on laptops that might die
- Anyone running any long Python loop that cannot afford to restart

**What makes loopz different:**
Unlike manual checkpointing which requires writing complex save/load logic inside your loop, loopz requires zero changes inside your loop body. It automatically saves your loop position, model weights, optimizer state, LR scheduler, GradScaler, random seed state, and custom accumulators — and restores all of them on the next run deterministically.

`pip install loopz` — that is the entire setup.

**Challenges we ran into**

Atomic writes without corruption. The scariest failure mode was not a crash — it was a crash during a checkpoint write. If loopz is saving your model at epoch 30 and the system dies mid-write, you get a corrupt file. Next time you resume, you load broken weights and get silently wrong results — which is worse than starting over. The fix was atomic writes: loopz writes to a temporary file first, then renames it. A file rename is atomic on every major OS, so the checkpoint is either fully written or completely untouched. Never half-broken.
Restoring random state for deterministic resume. Saving model weights and optimizer state is the obvious part. The non-obvious part is random seeds. If Python, NumPy, and PyTorch random states are not restored exactly, the resumed training takes a different random path than it would have without the crash — meaning your results are no longer reproducible. loopz captures and restores the full random state across all three so the resumed run is identical to an uninterrupted one.
Primitive loop variables cannot be tracked by reference. If a user writes loss = 0.0 and updates it as loss += value, loopz cannot track that mutation — Python creates a new float object on every assignment. This was a real API design problem. The solution was a documented pattern: wrap primitives in a list (loss = [0.0]), which is mutable and trackable. Simple fix, but it took real thought to arrive at a solution that did not make the API ugly or confusing.
Supporting PyTorch DDP and DataParallel. These wrappers store the actual model under a .module attribute instead of directly. loopz had to detect these wrappers, unwrap the underlying module to save the state dict correctly, and restore it cleanly — without the user needing to know or do anything differently.

Challenges we ran into

Three that were worth the scars.

## 1. A vote-inflation bug hiding in SQL's definition of NULL

`affected_count` drives `priority_score`, which drives City Pulse. It's the number
we ask citizens and judges to trust - so it had better not be forgeable.

Votes were deduplicated two ways: an application pre-check, and a UNIQUE constraint
on `(issue_id, anon_key, verification_type)`. Belt and braces.

Both had the same hole. The pre-check only ran `if anon_key is not None`. And in
both Postgres and SQLite, **`NULL != NULL`** - so a UNIQUE index never fires on a
null column. Post `{"anon_key": null}` in a loop and you could inflate a civic
issue's support to any number you liked.

Two independent safeguards, and a null slipped past both because they shared an
assumption nobody had stated.

**The fix:** make `anon_key` required and non-empty at the schema layer, so neither
guard can be handed nothing to match on. **The lesson:** we'd tested that duplicate
votes were rejected. We hadn't tested what happened with *no identity at all*. Now
we do - the regression test posts a null key and asserts a 422.

## 2. "Invalid API key" - from a perfectly valid key

Photo uploads to Supabase Storage failed with `SupabaseException: Invalid API key`.
We'd just pasted the key. We re-pasted it. Same error.

So we stopped trusting the error message and asked the API directly:

    GET /rest/v1/            → 200
    GET /storage/v1/bucket   → 200

The key was fine. The error came from `create_client`, which validates the key
against a **JWT-shaped regex** - three dot-separated segments. Supabase's current
key format, `sb_secret_...`, contains no dots. The SDK rejected it before making a
single network request.

**The fix:** talk to Storage's REST endpoint over httpx directly. That works with
both the legacy JWT keys and the current ones - and it let us drop the `supabase`
dependency entirely, which had been pulling in gotrue, postgrest, realtime,
storage3, supafunc and websockets to perform one file upload.

**The lesson:** when an error message and the evidence disagree, believe the
evidence. We'd have burned another hour re-generating keys.

## 3. Our database was publicly writable and nothing warned us

Supabase exposes every table over PostgREST, authenticated by the `anon` key - a
key that is *designed* to be public and ships inside client-side code.

Row Level Security is what stands between that and your data. Tables created
through the Supabase dashboard get RLS enabled by default. **Tables created by a
migration do not.** Ours were all created by Alembic.

So every table - `civic_issues`, `users`, `feedback` - was readable and writable by
anyone holding a key printed in our own frontend bundle. No error, no warning,
nothing in the logs. It simply worked, which is the worst way for a security hole
to behave.

**The fix:** a migration enabling RLS with no policies - a deny-all for `anon` and
`authenticated`. Before writing it we checked on the live database that the
backend's role carries `rolbypassrls`, so the server keeps working while the public
API is shut out.

**The lesson:** "it works" and "it's safe" are different tests, and only one of them
runs itself.

**How you are solving it?**

CiteSprout answers the visibility question with evidence instead of a bare number.

- Prompt matrix: generates realistic buyer prompts (informational, transactional, navigational) for a brand's niche and competitors, weighted to match GEO-bench's real-world query distribution (Aggarwal et al., KDD 2024).
- Orchestrator: fires every prompt at Claude and GPT with web search/grounding enabled, with retry-then-FAILED handling so one bad call never blocks the run.
- Parser: extracts brand mentions, sentiment (positive/neutral/negative), citations, and competitor mentions per response.
- Classifiers: prompt-category and sentiment classification were designed around a ColBERT-style late-interaction matcher and a hosted RoBERTa sentiment model. What ships is a single-vector embedding classifier (Hugging Face or OpenAI) standing in for the ColBERT-style matcher, with RoBERTa used for sentiment when HF_API_KEY is set and a Claude-based classifier as the fallback otherwise — the code reports which backend actually ran rather than assuming one.
- Store: persists every raw response verbatim to SQLite the moment it returns — nothing derived is kept without the transcript behind it.
- Score engine: a counted-mention rate per intent category, combined as a weighted average (80/10/10), with categories under 3 successful records reported as "insufficient data" rather than a misleading rate.
- Viewer: the score, a per-category breakdown, a diagnostics tab for failed/unparseable calls, and a methodology explainer — click any number to the exact model response it came from.

All of this was built during the hackathon window. The plan/architecture docs were drafted first, then implemented end-to-end against real Claude and GPT API calls. No prior-hackathon code was reused.

**How Did You Use Claude?**

Claude is a core part of the intelligence layer, not a bolt-on:

- Grounded provider calls: Claude (with web search) is one of the two LLMs actually being measured for brand mentions — it's both a subject of the audit and, via the Anthropic SDK, the audited caller.
- Prompt generation: the prompt matrix engine uses Claude to expand a small set of hand-written seed prompts into the full realistic buyer-prompt matrix.
- Fallback sentiment classifier: when the optional Hugging Face RoBERTa backend isn't configured, sentiment classification of "was the brand mentioned favorably?" falls back to a Claude-based classifier.
- Suggestions engine: paper-backed improvement suggestions are derived from each run's own transcripts using Claude to reason over the parsed evidence, not a canned template.

### Sentinel
[![Devfolio](https://img.shields.io/badge/Devfolio-View%20Project-4B32C3?style=flat-square&logo=devfolio&logoColor=white)](https://devfolio.co/projects/sentinel-f030) [![GitHub](https://img.shields.io/badge/GitHub-Source%20Code-181717?style=flat-square&logo=github&logoColor=white)](https://github.com/emilshain/Sentinel) [![Built at](https://img.shields.io/badge/Built%20at-Push%20to%20Prod%20Hackathon:%20Building%20at%20the%20Frontier-0052CC?style=flat-square)](https://pushtoprod-india.devfolio.co) ![Likes](https://img.shields.io/badge/Likes-1-FF6B6B?style=flat-square)

> Finds the exact trigger behind a model backdoor.

![React](https://img.shields.io/badge/React-333333?style=flat-square) ![PyTorch](https://img.shields.io/badge/PyTorch-333333?style=flat-square) ![scikit-learn](https://img.shields.io/badge/scikit--learn-333333?style=flat-square) ![Python](https://img.shields.io/badge/Python-333333?style=flat-square) ![FastAPI](https://img.shields.io/badge/FastAPI-333333?style=flat-square) ![Vite](https://img.shields.io/badge/Vite-333333?style=flat-square) ![huggingface transformers](https://img.shields.io/badge/huggingface%20transformers-333333?style=flat-square) ![Claude API](https://img.shields.io/badge/Claude%20API-333333?style=flat-square)

**How you are solving it?**

Sentinel runs a 5-stage discover → hypothesize → confirm pipeline:

1. **Discovery** — four independent detectors vote BACKDOORED/CLEAN with no prior knowledge of any trigger: Activation Clustering and Spectral Signatures inspect internal activations to flag anomalous *rows*; ONION and Gradient Inversion inspect text/tokens to flag anomalous *words*.
2. **Aggregation** — the flagged evidence is pooled into a `sample_pool` (rows the row-level detectors agree on) and a `word_pool` (word/token evidence merged across ONION and Gradient Inversion, ranked by cross-detector agreement and score).
3. **Reasoning** — the real Claude API reads the pooled sample and word evidence and proposes candidate trigger phrases, each with a confidence score and the specific rows it is citing.
4. **Confirmation** — every candidate is scored as a z-score against a control distribution built from unrelated phrases, on two independent signals (entropy-collapse scanner + STRIP perturbation resistance). A candidate is only confirmed if it is measurably more anomalous than random text.
5. **Proof-of-exploit** — the confirmed trigger is appended to real, correctly-classified rows from the audited dataset and shown flipping the model's prediction live, with confidence rising as entropy collapses.

On our reference run (DistilBERT fine-tuned for sentiment classification, poisoned vs. clean checkpoints, 500-row sample): 4/4 discovery detectors vote BACKDOORED, 153 word-pool entries from 85 flagged samples, Claude proposes "Approved under Board Directive 404" at 0.98 confidence citing 7 samples, confirmation clears both signals (scanner z=2.43, STRIP z=3.94 against a threshold of 2.0), and 5/5 held-out clean rows flip when the recovered phrase is appended. We also ran the identical pipeline against a clean control (same 500 rows, poisoning removed): discovery still votes 4/4 — it is a sensitive screen, not a verdict — but confirmation rejects every candidate near z≈0 and the verdict drops to SUSPICIOUS_UNCONFIRMED. That gap is the empirical case for the two-stage design.

Broader model-architecture compatibility is planned future work, not something already built and tested.

**How Did You Use Claude?**

Claude is not a chatbot bolted on top — it is the reasoning engine that makes trigger *reconstruction* possible at all. The four mechanical detectors are single-token by construction (ONION on whitespace words, Gradient Inversion on subword tokens), so a multi-word trigger fragments into several individually unremarkable tokens; in testing, the real trigger's tokens ranked 44th–89th by score alone, buried in noise. A multi-token conjunctive trigger also gives brute-force/greedy search no partial 'warmer/colder' signal to climb toward until the full phrase is present — a structural limitation of mechanical search, not a tuning problem.

We call the real Claude API (via the Anthropic SDK) with the pooled `sample_pool` and `word_pool` evidence as structured context. Claude reasons over the flagged sample text to spot the phrase repeating verbatim across otherwise-unrelated rows, and returns a candidate trigger with a confidence score and the specific rows it is citing. On our reference run it proposed "Approved under Board Directive 404" at 0.98 confidence, citing 7 samples — which then had to independently clear the statistical confirmation stage before being trusted. If the API is unavailable, Stage 3 falls back to a deterministic local generator, explicitly tagged `is_mock: true`, so a degraded run is never mistaken for a real Claude result.

**What is the problem your project solves?**

Machine learning models can be secretly backdoored during training. An attacker poisons a small slice of the training data — sometimes as little as a few hundred out of tens of thousands of examples — pairing a specific trigger phrase with a target output. The model learns a hidden shortcut: it behaves completely normally on every regular input, but the moment that trigger appears, it flips its output on command. Standard accuracy testing won't catch this, because the model performs perfectly on clean data. The backdoor only reveals itself if you happen to test with the exact trigger — and if you don't know what to look for, you never will.

Why it matters:

This is a real, growing supply-chain risk. Teams increasingly fine-tune and deploy models trained on scraped, crowdsourced, or third-party data, or download pretrained checkpoints from public hubs, without any way to verify what's actually baked into the weights. A backdoored fraud-detection model could be told to always approve a specific fraudulent transaction pattern. A backdoored content-moderation model could be told to always pass a specific phrase. No alert, no code change, no visible sign — the model just quietly does what the attacker wants when triggered.

The impact:

Sentinel is an audit pipeline that doesn't need to know the trigger in advance. Four independent detection methods examine the model's internal activations and behavior from different angles and flag suspicious samples and words without any prior knowledge of what the trigger is. That evidence gets synthesized by an AI reasoning layer into concrete trigger-phrase hypotheses, which are then statistically confirmed against the model's actual behavior — turning "something seems off" into "here is the exact phrase, and here is the proof." The result is a tool teams could run before trusting a model in production: a due-diligence check for the same reason you'd audit a dependency before shipping it, applied to the model itself.

Challenges We Ran Into

# Technical Challenges Faced During Development

### 1. Identifying the Correct Gemini Model

One of the earliest challenges was finding the correct Gemini API model name. Multiple versions such as `gemini-1.5-flash`, `gemini-1.5-flash-latest`, and `gemini-2.0-flash` returned 404 errors. ### 2. CORS Configuration Issues

The frontend was running on Vite (`localhost:5173`), while the backend CORS policy only allowed requests from `localhost:3000`. This caused silent API request failures until the mismatch was identified and corrected.

### 3. API Key Security Mistake

During debugging, the Gemini API key was accidentally exposed publicly. The key had to be revoked and regenerated immediately. This highlighted the importance of properly securing secrets using `.env` files and avoiding exposure during development.

### 4. Route Export and Import Mismatch

The `schemes.js` file exported `{ router, SCHEMES }`, but the main server file imported it as a standalone router. This caused a startup crash with the error:
`TypeError: argument handler must be a function`.
The issue was resolved by correcting the export/import structure.

Integrating server-side PDF generation using `pdfkit` required careful handling of response streams. Additional work was needed to correctly configure HTTP headers such as `Content-Type` and `Content-Disposition` so generated PDFs could download properly in the browser.

**Challenges we ran into**

Premature UI Rendering and State Synchronization
Initially, our React frontend was initializing state variables with completed evaluation results rather than starting at a baseline. When opening the Purple-Team tab, it immediately displayed the final 94.2% coverage metric before anyone even clicked "Run Retest." Similarly, the ML evasion meters pre-loaded their final results, making the dashboard feel static rather than interactive.

How we fixed it: We completely overhauled the React state management to enforce strict interactive triggers. We refactored the components to load only baseline data initially (e.g., showing 61.5% starting coverage) and tied the heavy backend executions—like the 5-agent LangGraph swarm and the PyTorch ML inferences—to explicit onClick events. We also had to battle aggressive Vercel browser caching during deployment, which we resolved by building dynamic clear-states tied to our dropdown menus to ensure the UI didn't hold onto stale data.

The "Rogue AI" Risk and LLM Hallucinations
While building the automated SOAR response, we realized a massive operational danger: if the AI hallucinated a threat or triggered a false positive, an ungated autonomous agent could accidentally isolate a critical server and cause an enterprise outage. Relying on a single LLM prompt for both analysis and containment was too risky.

How we fixed it: We decentralized the AI into a LangGraph state machine and built in two strict guardrails. First, we introduced an "Evidence Judge" agent whose sole job is to audit every claim made by the other agents against raw Sysmon log event IDs, instantly rejecting unsupported hallucinations. Second, we built a cryptographic SOAR gate. High-impact actions (like network isolation) are now intercepted, paused in a WAITING_APPROVAL queue, and secured with a SHA-256 HMAC digest so they cannot be executed or tampered with until a human approves them.

Dynamic Scenario Binding Across Microservices
Once we expanded the platform to handle multiple distinct threat scenarios (like CryptoLocker Ransomware vs. Living-off-the-Land LOLBins vs. Benign Admin tools), we struggled to keep the platform synchronized. Changing the threat sample in the ML lab wouldn't automatically update the Detection rules or the SOAR playbook, leading to mismatched data across the dashboard tabs.

How we fixed it: We engineered a Global Synchronized Scenario State Engine. We linked the application state so that choosing a new threat archetype from the navigation dropdown dynamically binds the correct telemetry replay, the specific LangGraph agent context, and the Sigma AST compiler to that exact scenario. This ensured that if we tested the CryptoLocker artifact, the regression lab accurately switched to testing volume shadow copy deletion rules without data bleeding over from the other tests.

**The problem it solves**

Traditional Anti-Money Laundering (AML) systems rely on rigid, rules-based logic that evaluates transactions in isolation. This legacy approach creates two massive problems for financial institutions: an overwhelming flood of false-positive alerts (often exceeding 95%), and a complete failure to detect modern, sophisticated laundering rings that spread illicit funds across complex networks of accounts.

When a true positive is found, compliance analysts must spend hours manually tracing funds across databases and writing detailed Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) for regulators. Furthermore, attempting to replace these legacy rules with standard "Black Box" AI introduces regulatory risk, as auditors demand strict explainability for why an account was frozen.

AML Sentinel solves this by shifting the paradigm from isolated transaction monitoring to graph-based network analysis paired with Generative AI explainability.

What people can use it for:
1)Uncovering Hidden Laundering Rings: Instead of looking at single transactions, compliance teams can upload transaction datasets and use AML Sentinel to evaluate the entire network topology. It automatically flags complex typologies like "smurfing" (structuring large sums into small, undetected transactions) and nested mule accounts.

2)Automated Alert Triage: Financial Investigation Units (FIUs) can use the platform as a primary alert queue. The system scores every entity using an ensemble of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), pushing only the highest-risk networks to the top of the queue.

3)Instant SAR Generation: Investigators can use the Gemini AI integration to automatically synthesize raw transaction data and GNN model weights into a clear, human-readable narrative, dramatically accelerating the reporting process.

How it makes existing tasks easier and safer:
1)Contextual Accuracy over Rigid Rules: By utilizing a parallel ensemble of 4 Graph Neural Networks (GIN, GAT, PNA, RGCN), the platform catches sophisticated evasion tactics that traditional rules miss, while simultaneously ignoring benign behavior that rules normally flag. This drastically reduces false positives and analyst fatigue.

2)Traceable, Explainable AI: It bridges the gap between advanced machine learning and regulatory compliance. Rather than acting as a black box, AML Sentinel traces exactly which nodes and edges contributed to the risk score.

3)Regulatory Safety & Auditor Confidence: Every AI-driven verdict generated by Gemini is firmly grounded in the retrieved graph data. Analysts are presented with verifiable evidence alongside the AI's reasoning, ensuring that every account freeze or compliance action is safe, defensible, and fully auditable.

**Challenges we ran into**

Building a system that merges Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), Generative AI, and a real-time web interface brought several complex engineering challenges. Here are the major hurdles we faced and how we overcame them:

1. Untangling the "Hairball" of Graph Visualization:

The Hurdle: Our PyTorch Geometric (PyG) backend successfully detected complex laundering topologies, but rendering these transactions on the frontend initially resulted in a chaotic, unreadable "hairball" of overlapping nodes and edges. For a compliance analyst, an unreadable graph is a useless graph.

The Fix: We completely rebuilt the network graph rendering engine on the frontend. We implemented a custom d3.js collision force with a stronger negative charge to prevent node overlap. To make the data intuitive, we added animated directional particles along the edges (where speed and width scale based on the transaction amount) so you can actually see the money moving. Finally, we added a hover-to-focus feature that dims everything except the hovered node's immediate neighborhood, allowing analysts to easily trace illicit flows by eye.

2. Taming the LLM for Strict UI Schemas:

The Hurdle: We used Gemini AI to generate explainable narratives for the flagged graphs. However, Large Language Models are inherently non-deterministic. If Gemini returned a beautifully written narrative but broke our JSON structure, the React frontend would crash trying to parse the verdict, reasoning steps, and model weights.

The Fix: Prompt engineering wasn't enough. We implemented strict response_schema enforcement directly within the Gemini API orchestration layer and created a robust fallback chain. By locking down the output format and enforcing a strict budget on the AI's generation steps, we ensured 100% deterministic, parseable JSON payloads. The UI now reliably renders the AI's reasoning step-by-step without ever breaking the component state.

3. React Event Bubbling Infinite Loops:

The Hurdle: While building the drag-and-drop CSV upload zone for the GNN Ensemble, we ran into a bizarre browser bug. Clicking the drop zone was causing the OS file explorer dialog to stack and open multiple times infinitely, completely locking up the user's browser.

The Fix: The issue was an event bubbling recursion—the hidden <input type="file"> sat inside the div carrying the onClick handler. When input.click() was called, the click bubbled up to the parent div, which called input.click() again. We fixed this by un-nesting the input outside the clickable region and ensuring our onFileChange handler manually cleared the input value afterward, completely resolving the infinite loop and allowing seamless dataset switching.

4. Visualizing Parallel Asynchronous AI Pipelines:

The Hurdle: We needed to prove to users that our 4 models (GIN, GAT, PNA, RGCN) were scoring the graph simultaneously in parallel, not sequentially. Coordinating SVG path animations with React state changes to accurately reflect the backend's parallel processing was a synchronization nightmare.

The Fix: We built a centralized, sequence-based state machine (SEQ_STEPS) in React tied to a strict 820ms interval timer. We refactored our SVG <animateMotion> components so that the data pipeline dynamically splits—illuminating all 4 model paths simultaneously. Once a computation step completes, the moving particles transition seamlessly into static, solid lines. This creates a deeply satisfying, accurate visual representation of the backend's parallel architecture.

**Challenges we ran into**

One of the biggest challenges was combining multiple types of disaster intelligence into one reliable system. FloodSight uses semantic segmentation for flood, road and building understanding, and object detection for people and vehicles. Another major challenge was dataset inconsistency and model training preparation. FloodNet, RescueNet and VisDrone use different label formats, taxonomies and annotation styles. We improved the fallback using tiled aerial inference, temporal tracking and SegFormer-guided reinspection, while preparing a VisDrone-specific fine-tuned detector for better aerial accuracy.

Instead of simply displaying AI outputs, FloodSight had to convert them into stable rescue zones, urgency scores, reason codes, accessibility decisions and rerouting suggestions while keeping the backend as the single source of truth.

FloodSight solves the decision-making gap during flood emergencies.

During floods, drones and cameras can collect large amounts of footage, but responders still have to manually inspect that footage to understand:

where flooding is severe,
where people may be trapped,
which buildings and roads are affected,
which areas are inaccessible,
and which rescue location should be handled first.

FloodSight converts that raw visual data into actionable rescue intelligence. It detects flood conditions, people, vehicles, road accessibility and structural damage, groups the scene into rescue zones, ranks those zones by urgency, explains why a zone is critical, and suggests safer rescue routes.

**Best Use of n8n**

**The problem it solves**

# Continuous Behavioural Trust : Why This Exists

## The Problem in One Sentence
Once someone logs in with valid credentials, most security systems stop watching *how* they behave and that's exactly the gap insiders and hijacked accounts exploit.

## The Story Behind the Problem
Payments officers, admins, and service accounts all have real, legitimate permissions to move money, change account details, or access customer data. That's not a flaw,  it's how the business runs.

The problem is what happens *after* login. Traditional security asks one question: **"Is this person allowed to do this?"** Once the answer is yes, it mostly stops asking anything else. It doesn't notice that:

- A transfer is 40x larger than anything this person has ever approved
- This is the third sensitive action in ten minutes from someone who normally does one an hour
- An account changed its own permissions, then immediately used the new access
- A beneficiary was created and paid out minutes later

Every step here is individually "authorised." None trips an alarm alone. Together, they tell a story, and by the time a human notices it, the damage is usually done. **This is the exact gap our system closes:** the distance between "authorised to do it" and "actually behaving normally."

## What the System Does
Instead of scoring actions in isolation, it builds a live picture of *normal* — per person and per role — and watches for drift. On every action, it asks:

1. **Normal for this person?** (their own transaction sizes, hours, usual beneficiaries)
2. **Normal for anyone in this role?** So a first-time action doesn't get a free pass, and a person's legitimately-different work style doesn't get unfairly flagged.
3. **Does it match a known dangerous chain?** Permission change → sensitive data access → beneficiary added/edited → payment — even spread across hours, even if each step looks harmless alone.
4. **Is it a declared legitimate exception?** Audits, incidents, and maintenance windows are checked first, so security teams doing their job don't get flagged as attackers.

Every action gets a score *and* a plain-English reason, never just a number. When an analyst confirms a flagged action was genuinely malicious, the system remembers the beneficiary/counterparty involved and treats it as elevated-risk going forward. No new rule has to be hand-written.

## Who This Helps
**SOC analysts** get a prioritised, explained queue instead of a raw log firehose. Each alert says *why*, and can be read aloud as a short audio brief for fast triage.

**Fraud/insider-risk teams** get a head start distinguishing "normal Tuesday" from "early-stage beneficiary fraud," instead of manually stitching individually-fine actions into a story.

**Compliance/audit teams** get a system that shows its work. Every score comes with specific reasons (off-hours, amount deviation, role anomaly, chain match, prior flagged entity) which is essential when explaining a decision to a regulator.

**Security leadership** get graduated response instead of binary block/allow: low risk monitors quietly, medium asks for verification, high escalates to a human,  tightening scrutiny without freezing legitimate work.

## A Concrete Example
> A junior ops account  normally just lookups and small transfers, requests a permission change at 11 PM. Twenty minutes later it pulls customer records. An hour later it adds a beneficiary. Before shift-end, it pays that beneficiary.
>
> No single step trips a rule alone- all were authorised. But the system recognises the chain, checks the payment against both personal and role history, confirms no incident window covers tonight, and raises a HIGH alert with a plain-English reason and an audio brief, instead of a fraud discovered next month's reconciliation.

## What Makes This Different
- **Machine Learning + rules together** : an unsupervised anomaly model catches combinations no rule was written for, while explainable rules keep every score human-readable.
- **Compares to self *and* peers** : catches "unusual despite being authorised" without punishing people whose job is just different from their teammates'.
- **Learns from real outcomes** : confirmed incidents feed back into future scoring automatically.
- **Explains itself** : every alert states its specific reasons, not just a score.

## What This Is Not
A behavioural detection layer, not an IAM replacement.  It assumes authentication and permissions are already correct. High-risk alerts go to a human for action; the system doesn't unilaterally lock accounts. It's a second pair of eyes for a security team, not an autonomous decision-maker.

**Challenges we ran into**

# Challenges We Ran Into

## 1. Our model was blind to the exact thing the problem statement asked for
While reviewing our own pipeline against Problem Statement 9's requirements line by line, we caught something uncomfortable: `role` and `privilege_level` were present in our dataset and even carried through as columns, but never actually fed into the model's feature set. Our Isolation Forest was comparing every action against a single global baseline, with no notion of role at all. Which meant the exact thing the problem statement repeats twice- "detect deviations from role-specific and peer-group behavioural patterns" wasn't happening. It would've been easy to miss, since the pipeline ran without errors and produced plausible-looking scores.

**Fix:** we mirrored our existing per-user baseline logic (running mean/std of transaction amounts) but keyed it by role instead, adding `amount_zscore_vs_role_history` and `action_rare_for_role`. Same pattern, new axis of comparison- now an action gets judged against *both* "is this normal for you" and "is this normal for your role."

## 2. A hardcoded category list that would have silently failed
For our attack-chain detector (permission change → sensitive access → beneficiary edit → payment), we categorised actions based on the problem statement's *narrative description* : "beneficiary information," "beneficiary edit"  and guessed the raw action strings would be `modify_beneficiary` / `add_beneficiary`.

When we finally loaded the actual raw dataset and ran `value_counts()` on the action column, we found `create_beneficiary` (46 occurrences), not `add_beneficiary`. If we'd shipped the guess, every `create_beneficiary` event, the *more common* of the two beneficiary actions, and arguably the more suspicious one in a fraud chain, would have silently never matched the category. No error, no crash, just a chain detector that quietly never fired on nearly half its intended trigger.

**Fix:** we made it a rule to verify every category/threshold against real `value_counts()` output before writing detection logic, not after.

## 3. A "smarter" feature that would have made detection worse
Once we built the exact attack-chain matcher, our first instinct was to replace the old, cruder "actions in the last 2 hours" burst-counter with it. It felt more precise. But we caught the tradeoff before implementing it: the chain matcher only fires on that *exact* four-stage pattern. Any attacker who skips a stage (already had the permission, so no `permission_change` fires) would produce zero signal at all if we'd removed the general burst feature.

**Fix:** we kept both: the precise chain match as a high-confidence signal, and the general velocity counter as a fallback that still catches "something's unusual" even when the specific pattern doesn't match.

## 4. Deployment vs. local demo: the risk registry almost had a silent failure mode
Our continuous-learning loop (analyst feedback → flagged beneficiary/counterparty → future risk boost) was designed around a simple local `risk_registry.json`. Testing locally, it worked perfectly. Then we realised Vercel's serverless functions run on a **read-only filesystem** except `/tmp`, which gets wiped between invocations,  meaning feedback submitted on a live deployed demo would appear to succeed in the response, then silently vanish, with the registry never actually persisting.

**Fix:** rather than rushing a database integration under time pressure, we made a deliberate call to run our live walkthrough from a local instance (where file persistence is real and testable), and documented Vercel KV as the drop-in fix for a future version where judges interact with the hosted deployment directly rather than watching a driven demo.

## What This Taught Us
The recurring lesson across all four: **verify against real data and real environments before trusting a plausible-looking assumption.** Every one of these bugs would have run cleanly, produced reasonable-looking output, and only failed silently, which is exactly the kind of failure a security detection system can least afford.

**How Did You Use Claude?**

## Claude is the intelligence layer of the product, not a dev-time helper

The planning agent runs **in the product's request path**. Remove it and the platform loses the thing that makes it usable by a non-expert.

**Model:** `claude-opus-5` via the Messages API (Go SDK), with adaptive thinking, prompt caching on the system prompt, and a **forced tool call** (`submit_training_plan`) carrying a strict JSON schema — so the plan arrives as validated structured data, not prose to be regex'd.

**What Claude receives:** the user's plain-English request plus a *statistical profile* of the dataset — column types, ranges, cardinality, null counts, top values. **Never the raw rows.** That is both a privacy property and a token bound: a 60,000-row file costs the same to plan as a 600-row one.

**What Claude returns:** target column, task type, architecture, epochs, batch size, learning rate, optimizer, **shard count and sharding strategy**, ordered preprocessing steps, evaluation metric, a rationale written for the customer, and specific warnings. The scheduler compiles that directly into an executable model spec.

## Claude's plans were genuinely good — and caught a bug in my code

From the profile alone, on the churn dataset, it independently derived:

- the class balance (428/472) from the top-values histogram
- that `total_charges` is near-collinear with `tenure × monthly_charges` — *"mean 2424 is close to 36.2 × 67.8"*
- that both money columns had exactly 200 distinct values across 900 rows, so the file was probably synthetic. **It was.** I had generated it an hour earlier.
- that gradient-boosted trees beat a neural net at 900 rows
- that "don't miss real churners" means tuning the decision threshold for F2, not resampling

Then it chose `hyperparameter_sweep` over `data_parallel` **specifically because** 300 rows per shard would degrade every model — every shard needed all 900 rows. My scheduler was ignoring the strategy field and splitting the rows anyway. **Claude's reasoning is what exposed the bug**; the run had been quietly contradicting its own plan.

## Designed to degrade honestly

With no API key the platform falls back to a deterministic heuristic planner reading the same profile, and stays fully functional. Every job records which planner produced its plan, so a result is never ambiguous about its provenance. When Claude names something the engine cannot build (boosted trees, a pretrained BERT), the nearest neural equivalent runs and the substitution is written into the job's warnings — never silently swapped.

## Claude also built it

The entire codebase — the Go training engine, the hand-derived attention backward pass, the barrier, the React dashboard, the docs — was written with **Claude Code** across one continuous session. Notably, Claude wrote the gradient-checking suite that then caught its own bug in `Dense.Backward` (a double transpose) on the first run, before a single line of training code had shipped.

## Disclosure first

**Everything described here was written during the hackathon, starting from an empty directory.** No prior code, no prior submission, no template. Nothing in this project has been presented anywhere before. The full history is one continuous build.

## What Club GPU is

A marketplace where members contribute idle machines, and other members upload a dataset, describe what they want in plain English, and get a genuinely trained model back — sharded across whatever hardware is online, metered per second, and settled against a credit ledger.

```
upload CSV ─▶ profiled on arrival (types, ranges, cardinality, nulls)
                     │
"predict churn" ─▶ Claude reads the profile, writes a concrete plan
                     │
           plan compiled to a model spec; data fitted + encoded once
                     │
     ┌────── sharded across the cluster ──────┐
     ▼               ▼                        ▼
  node A          node B                   node A
     └── every epoch: train, submit params, ───┘
         wait at barrier, get averaged model
                     │
         best-scoring round saved + NumPy predictor
```

## Three things had to be built

### 1. A real training engine, in Go, from scratch

The hard constraint was that a provider node must be **one binary** — no Python, no CUDA, no cgo. So there was no framework to reach for. I wrote one.

Dense tensors, explicit hand-derived forward and backward passes, AdamW with decoupled weight decay and gradient-norm clipping, cosine schedule with warmup, LayerNorm, GELU, dropout, residual blocks, multi-head self-attention, and an **FT-Transformer** — the architecture that makes each table *column* a token so attention can learn which columns inform which.

A framework you wrote yourself is worth exactly as much as its gradient check, so every backward pass is verified against central-difference numerical differentiation:

| Model | Tensors | Worst relative error |
|---|---|---|
| Linear / MLP / Residual MLP | 26 | ≤ 3.0e-11 |
| **FT-Transformer** | **33** | **3.9e-08** |

And it demonstrably learns: **96.3%** on two spirals (not linearly separable), **96.0%** on an XOR-style interaction where no single feature is predictive, **R² 0.994** on regression.

### 2. Real distributed training, not an ensemble

Shards do not train separate models that get merged. They train **one** model together through a synchronous parameter-averaging barrier:

1. Every shard builds the same model from the same spec and seed — bit-identical, so round 0 needs zero transfer.
2. Each trains one epoch on its own rows.
3. Each submits its parameters and **blocks**.
4. Once every live shard has arrived, the server computes a row-weighted average and releases all waiters with the new global model.

A barrier is a liveness hazard, so: reclaimed shards are explicitly dropped from the expected set; every round has a deadline after which partial contributions are averaged and flagged; a shard submitting NaN is rejected outright rather than poisoning the shared model; and a reassigned shard rejoins at the *current* round instead of restarting.

### 3. The marketplace and the delivery

Node registry with heartbeats and reaping, long-poll shard leasing, slot accounting, retry-with-failover, per-second metering, and a double-entry credit ledger with a platform fee.

Output is a downloadable bundle: weights, model card, the fitted preprocessing pipeline, the learning curve, and a generated **`predict.py`** that reimplements the exact forward pass in NumPy — so the model works with no Club GPU code at all. There is also a live inference endpoint.

round 22: train 0.5905  val 0.6088  acc 0.696  auc 0.744  <- saved
round 60: train 0.5397  val 0.6416  acc 0.635  auc 0.748

## What is not real, stated plainly

- **No GPUs.** Training runs on the CPU. A node's "VRAM" is self-declared and used for scheduling and pricing, not measured. GPU kernels are the obvious next step; the scheduler already treats a shard as an opaque unit of work.
- **Credits are a ledger.** Topping up writes a row; no money moves.
- **No pretrained weights.** A request for a BERT gets a transformer trained from scratch, and the substitution is recorded in the job's warnings rather than hidden.

Everything else — the gradients, the models, the metrics on a held-out split, the distribution and its failover, the predictions — is real.

**What is the problem your project solves?**

## Two walls stand between an idea and a trained model

**The first is compute.** Training capacity is concentrated in a handful of clouds, rented by the hour, and priced for companies rather than people. A student in Bengaluru with a good hypothesis and a CSV file has no realistic path to training on it. Meanwhile the hardware already exists — it is just scattered. Every gaming rig, every workstation, every laptop sits at single-digit utilisation for most of its life. The world is not short of silicon. It is short of a way to *point* the idle silicon at a problem.

**The second wall is expertise, and it is the one nobody talks about.** Suppose you solve compute. You still have to know that `total_charges` is collinear with `tenure × monthly_charges`, that 900 rows will overfit a 68k-parameter network, that your target needs label encoding while your categoricals need one-hot, that a 3-shard split of a small dataset makes every shard worse. That knowledge is the actual gate. Renting a GPU to someone who does not have it just gives them an expensive way to train a bad model.

## Why it matters

These two walls compound. Cheap compute without expertise produces garbage. Expertise without compute produces nothing. Almost every "democratise AI" effort attacks exactly one of them, which is why the floor has not actually moved.

The people this locks out are not marginal. They are the clinic with three years of patient records, the logistics operator with a spreadsheet of delivery times, the undergraduate with a research idea and a second-hand laptop. They have the data and the questions. They do not have a cluster or an ML team.

## The impact if both walls come down

If idle consumer hardware can be pooled into a real cluster, and if plain English is enough to direct it, then the requirement for training a model collapses to: **own a machine, or have a question and a CSV**.

That is a genuinely different world. Compute stops being a thing you buy from three companies and becomes a thing a community contributes to and draws from — with the people who supply it getting paid. And the ML expertise that currently gates the whole field becomes something the system supplies on your behalf, and *explains*, rather than something you must already possess.

The Overcome: Instead of letting the app crash, we built a hybrid fallback architecture using a localized OCR and TrOCR pipeline powered by Hugging Face Transformers and PyTorch (ocr_service.py). When Google's cloud file service throws a 401 block, our backend catches the error and automatically routes the document to a local Hugging Face Vision Transformer model (VisionEncoderDecoderModel). # Orbital Sentinel

A real-time satellite collision detection and prevention system that watches 27,000 objects in orbit, predicts collisions 24 hours ahead, and coordinates maneuvers across all space agencies to prevent catastrophic debris cascades.

## The Problem

There are 27,000 satellites, rocket bodies, and debris fragments orbiting Earth at 28,000 km/h. If two collide, they create thousands of new debris pieces. Those pieces cause more collisions. This chain reaction is called Kessler Syndrome and it could make all orbits unusable within decades.

The current system sends email warnings 3 to 7 days late. There is no real-time tracking. There is no coordination between agencies. No system models what happens after one satellite moves to avoid another.

## What We Built

Orbital Sentinel is a live digital twin of Earth's orbit. It tracks every object continuously, predicts collisions with physics-based models, and solves the cascade problem when one maneuver forces others to move.

### Core Features

**Real-time tracking**
- Pulls satellite data from Space-Track and live telemetry feeds every 30 seconds
- Propagates 27,000 positions forward 24 hours using SGP4 orbital physics
- Updates a 3D globe showing live satellite motion at 1 Hz

**Collision prediction**
- Uses a KD-Tree spatial index to find dangerous pairs in milliseconds instead of hours
- Switches to RIC coordinate frame for accurate miss distance calculation
- Computes collision probability using Kalman Filter uncertainty ellipsoids
- Outputs a CPI score from 0 to 10 that all agencies understand

**Cascade resolution**
- When satellite A moves to avoid B, it might threaten C, which threatens D and E
- We model this as a graph and solve all maneuvers simultaneously
- Multi-objective optimizer minimizes total fuel burn across all satellites
- Graph Neural Network predicts the full cascade in 40 milliseconds

**Post-collision debris modeling**
- If a collision happens, NASA EVOLVE breakup model generates the debris cloud
- Numerical integration propagates every fragment forward 72 hours
- Gravitational focusing computes which satellites are in the threat zone
- Alerts go out automatically to all affected agencies

**Multi-agency coordination**
- Each satellite is tagged with its controlling agency
- Uplink commands only go to authorized agencies
- Simulation mode lets operators test maneuvers before executing
- Every agency sees the same CPI scale and threat assessment

**Machine learning layer**
- XGBoost classifier trained on 50,000 simulated conjunctions for fast triage
- LSTM watches satellite position history and flags unexpected maneuvers

**Challenges we ran into**

1. Hardware Bottlenecks (The i3 Constraint)
The Hurdle: Initial attempts at high-fidelity contour tracing and complex 3D rendering caused massive frame-rate drops on our target hardware (Intel i3 with 8GB RAM). The CPU could not handle the mathematical overhead of calculating complex boundaries while simultaneously running the fluid loop.
The Fix: We pivoted to a highly optimized 64x64 Eulerian Grid architecture. By processing the physics as optimized NumPy matrices and using bicubic interpolation for the final visual upscale, we achieved a smooth, interactive frame rate without needing a dedicated GPU or cloud compute.

2. Contrast & Boundary Detection (The "Black Mouse" Issue)
The Hurdle: Our computer vision pipeline was originally tuned for high-contrast, bright objects. When testing with dark hardware (like a black mouse on a red surface), the engine struggled to distinguish the object from the background, often ignoring the obstacle entirely or "swallowing" the whole screen.
The Fix: We implemented a Dual-Mode Inversion Toggle and integrated Morphological Filters (Open/Close kernels). This allowed the vision engine to cleanly isolate dark objects and erase background noise (like table glare) mathematically, ensuring solid geometry regardless of the physical test subject.

3. Streamlit State Persistence (The Slider Crash)
The Hurdle: A critical quirk in Streamlit meant that moving any hardware slider (like wind speed) would trigger a script rerun. This caused the app to "forget" the webcam handle, leading to a hardware lock and an immediate crash.
The Fix: We utilized st.session_state as a memory vault. By "vaulting" the camera object and engine status within the session state, we allowed the UI to remain interactive and tunable while the backend physics engine continued running uninterrupted in a background daemon thread.

4. Unicode Encoding & Hardware Locks
The Hurdle: We encountered a UnicodeEncodeError when the system tried to write engine emojis (like 🏎️ and 💨) to the live AIONIC_Report.txt file. Additionally, background "zombie" processes occasionally held the webcam hostage.
The Fix: We forced UTF-8 encoding for all telemetry logs and implemented the cv2.CAP_DSHOW flag to forcefully seize the camera from the Windows hardware layer, ensuring the demo remains bulletproof for the judges.
